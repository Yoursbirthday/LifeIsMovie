There is something humiliating in crying before another human being.

Not because tears are shameful—no, tears are perhaps the last honest thing a man possesses—but because, when you cry before someone you love, you are placing the ugliest and most helpless part of yourself into their hands. You are saying, without saying it: Look at me. I am hurt. I cannot carry this alone anymore. Please, for once, understand me.

And if they look at you and remain unchanged, what then?

What an absurd question, isn't it? As though the human heart were a machine and tears were supposed to produce some measurable result. Perhaps I was foolish enough to believe that if someone truly loved me, my pain would become their pain. Perhaps I expected too much. Or perhaps I expected only the smallest thing a human being can expect from another human being—that their suffering should matter.

But nothing changes.

The next day everything continues exactly as before. The same distance. The same irritation. The same coldness disguised as normality.

And then you begin to hate yourself for having cried.

You think: Why did I show them that? Why did I make myself so small before them?

Because there is a terrible humiliation in discovering that your vulnerability has not softened the person before you. It has merely made you more vulnerable.

And then comes another realization, even more unbearable: perhaps they never hated your tears. Perhaps they simply did not care enough about them.

What a monstrous thought.

And yet, once it enters the mind, how does one drive it out?

Every request I make seems to acquire the character of an order in their ears. Every attempt to explain my feelings becomes an irritation. Even when I speak ordinarily, they hear accusation. Even when I speak from affection, they hear negativity. Even when I am silent, perhaps they hear something hostile in the silence.

So what am I supposed to do?

Speak less?

Love less?

Feel less?

Perhaps that would make me easier to love.

There is a peculiar cruelty in having to become less of yourself in order to remain acceptable to the person you love.

And I have asked myself again and again: What exactly have I done to deserve this?

But immediately another voice inside me laughs.

“Deserve? Who told you that love is a court of law?”

Yes. Perhaps that is the absurdity of it. I keep presenting evidence. I remember every sacrifice, every time I stayed, every time I forgave, every time I swallowed my pride because I thought love demanded it. I count these things secretly, shamefully, as though somewhere there must exist a balance sheet proving that I was good enough.

And then I despise myself for keeping accounts.

Because the moment you begin calculating what you have given and what you have received, perhaps love has already died and only resentment remains to perform its funeral.

What hurts me most is not even her anger.

It is that her anger seems to have more reality than my love.

My worst moments become permanent. My temper becomes my identity. My mistakes become evidence. But the countless ordinary moments in which I loved her quietly—without demanding applause, without asking to be thanked—vanish as though they had never existed.

And before others?

Perhaps I have become something else entirely.

She shows them her gentleness, her patience, her beautiful side—the person everyone can admire. And somehow, when my name enters the story, I become the difficult one. The angry one. The negative one. The one who does not understand.

Perhaps they all believe it.

Perhaps I even believe it sometimes.

And this is the most frightening part: when everyone is told that you are the villain long enough, eventually you begin interrogating yourself as though you must have committed some crime you cannot remember.

You begin thinking:

Maybe I really am impossible to love.

Maybe I really do ruin everything.

Maybe all this pain is simply the price of being around me.

And yet, somewhere beneath all this self-contempt, there remains one stubborn question.

If I am truly so terrible, why did I love her so gently?

Why did I think about her when she was not there?

Why did I remember the smallest things?

Why did her sadness disturb my peace?

Why did her happiness feel like something that belonged partly to me?

And then comes the answer I fear most.

Because perhaps I was thinking about her all the time not because she was thinking about me, but because I had made her the center of a world in which I was no longer the center of my own life.

There it is.

The humiliating truth.

I expected her to live inside my mind because she lived there constantly.

But people do not owe us residence in their thoughts merely because we have built temples for them inside our own.

She may go through her day without wondering where I am, what I am feeling, whether I am hurt, whether I am waiting for her message.

And I may spend the same day thinking of nothing else.

Is that injustice?

No.

That is what makes it worse.

It is simply reality.

And reality, unlike love, does not care whether you are ready for it.

Perhaps this is why I keep talking, explaining, arguing, pleading. Not because I believe words will change her, but because I am terrified of the silence that will follow when I finally stop.

For if I stop asking to be understood, and she does not come looking for the meaning behind my silence, then I will know.

Not suspect.

Not fear.

Know.

And what will I do with that knowledge?

Perhaps nothing.

Perhaps I will wake tomorrow, go about my day, speak normally, laugh when necessary, and behave as though nothing has happened.

That is what human beings are strangely capable of.

We can discover that we are alone while still sitting beside the person we love.

We can be held by someone and feel untouched.

We can speak for hours and remain completely unheard.

And perhaps the darkest loneliness is not being abandoned.

It is remaining together after you have realized that you are already alone.
